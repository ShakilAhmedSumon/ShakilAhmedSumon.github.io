From prediction to support: building a first-year retention model

This note follows the full path of the project: from defining retention and preparing the data to training the model and planning what happens after a student appears on a review list.

Published
August 2026
Reading time
12 minutes
Topic
Student retention

This public case study uses broad data groups. It omits student-level records, internal identifiers, operational thresholds, and other protected details.

Framing the question

The question came before the model

We began with a practical question. Which first-year students may benefit from earlier support, and when can the university act?

The goal was not to label a student. The goal was to create time. Advisors and student-support teams already know how to help. The model can help them decide where to look first when time and capacity are limited.

We defined retention as a student returning for the next academic year. We also defined a prediction date. Every feature had to exist before that date. This prevented the model from learning from information that would not be available in practice.

The prediction is not the intervention. It is one input to a human decision about support.

Preparing the data

Organizing the data as a timeline

Institutional data rarely arrives as one clean table. We brought together records from several university systems. We aligned student identifiers, academic periods, course records, and outcome definitions.

We organized the information as a timeline. The model could see only what the institution knew at the prediction date. This made the training data look more like the future production run.

Before enrollment

Entry context

  • Academic preparation available at entry
  • Entry term and admitted program
  • Initial enrollment status
During the year

Enrollment behavior

  • Credit load and changes in credit load
  • Course adds, drops, and withdrawals
  • Continuity across academic periods
Academic progress

Momentum

  • Credits attempted and earned
  • Course completion patterns
  • Term performance and change over time
Institutional setting

Study context

  • College and program structure
  • Course sequence and subject mix
  • Cohort and academic calendar context

Protected attributes are useful for fairness review. They should not become shortcuts for deciding who deserves support.

Feature engineering

Turning records into measures of momentum

A raw record says that a course was attempted. A useful feature asks what that attempt means in the student’s sequence of progress.

We created features that summarized pace, change, and consistency. We kept the definitions readable. An advisor should be able to understand the basic signal even if the model combines many signals at once.

We checked missing values carefully. Missing information can describe a process, not a student. It may show that two systems update at different times. We documented those cases instead of treating every blank as the same thing.

We also removed leakage. A feature was excluded if it depended on an event that happened after the prediction date or directly revealed the outcome.

Model training

Training across cohorts

We trained on earlier student cohorts. We held out a more recent cohort for testing. A random split would have been easier, but it could mix academic periods and make the result look more stable than it really was.

We began with an interpretable baseline. We then compared it with more flexible tree-based methods. The strongest model was not automatically the most useful model. We also considered calibration, stability, explanation, and the effort required to maintain it.

Production

New students

The same definitions and transformations are applied at the planned run date.

Retention outcomes are imbalanced. Most students return. We addressed this during training and evaluation. We avoided treating overall accuracy as the main result, because a model can appear accurate while failing to identify the smaller group that needs attention.

Evaluation

How we evaluated the model

We looked at discrimination, calibration, and usefulness at a realistic outreach capacity. Each measure answers a different question.

A model can rank students well but produce poor probability estimates. It can perform well overall and poorly for a smaller student group. It can also identify far more students than a support team can contact. We examined these problems separately.

Ranking

Who appears earlier?

ROC-AUC and precision-recall measures show how well the model separates outcomes across thresholds.

Capacity

Who can we reach?

Precision and recall at a fixed review size connect model performance to available staff time.

Calibration

Do probabilities mean what they say?

Observed outcomes are compared with predicted ranges. This matters when risk bands guide action.

Fairness and stability

For whom does it work?

We compare errors across relevant student groups and academic periods. We look for unstable gaps.

The operating threshold is a policy choice. It should reflect capacity, consequences, and the kind of support being offered.

Execution plan

A practical run schedule

Our production plan uses a small number of scheduled runs. Each run has a purpose. We wait for key records to stabilize, but we do not wait so long that support arrives too late.

What happens after the model returns a list?

The model may identify more students than the team can support at once. We therefore connect the list to a clear handoff process.

Human follow-through

What happens after scoring

01

Validate

Check the run date, population, missing data, and score distribution.

02

Size

Select a review group that matches the real capacity of support teams.

03

Review

Let authorized staff add context and remove cases where contact is not appropriate.

04

Reach out

Use supportive language. Offer help. Do not tell a student that an algorithm judged them.

05

Learn

Record contact, response, referral, and outcome so the process can be evaluated.

The final list is shared only with people who have a legitimate student-support role. A score alone does not explain why a student may not return. Staff need context, clear referral options, and a way to document what they learn.

We also need a comparison strategy. Without one, it is difficult to know whether the outreach helped, whether the model merely found students who would have returned anyway, or whether a different intervention would have worked better.

Success is not the number of predictions produced. Success is whether the process helps students receive useful support.

Working notes

What we are carrying forward

01

Definitions are part of the model.

Cohorts, outcomes, dates, and exclusions shape the result before training begins.

02

Capacity changes evaluation.

The useful question is often performance within the number of students a team can review.

03

Implementation needs ownership.

Someone must own the run, the review, the outreach, the recordkeeping, and the follow-up.

04

Fairness is ongoing work.

Subgroup performance and intervention outcomes should be reviewed each cycle.

05

Simple explanations matter.

People act more carefully when they understand what a score can and cannot mean.

06

The model will drift.

Programs, policies, student behavior, and data systems change. Monitoring is part of deployment.

A model can help organize attention. It cannot replace the context held by students, advisors, and support teams. The useful work begins when those pieces are brought together carefully.
